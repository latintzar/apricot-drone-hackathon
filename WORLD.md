# The World You Are Joining

Ilian · A guide to the next two weeks and the year ahead · September 2026

You already have a useful combination: you study computing and AI, you have built projects, and you earned a drone qualification at seventeen. Your aunt's orchard gives you a real problem to work on. These two weeks are a chance to bring those things together.

This guide gives you a short course, a project to build, and people to learn from. By the end, aim to have your existing work on GitHub, an orchard survey you can demonstrate, and a clear way to introduce yourself. The project can grow as you learn.

Start today: watch the first video, call your aunt or uncle about the drone, and choose one existing project to put online.

## 1. The world around your work

### A few useful words

**Startup.** A young company trying to find a product that can serve a large market. It learns by building things and seeing who uses them. Some use money from investors; others grow from customer payments.

**Scaleup.** A company that has found demand and is growing its team, sales and operations. Engineers work on making a useful product reliable for many more people.

**Venture capital, or VC.** Investment in companies that could grow a great deal. Investors receive ownership, called equity. Funding pays for future work; revenue is money customers pay for the product. A fund's portfolio is the list of companies it has invested in.

**Accelerator and batch.** An accelerator supports a group of companies through a programme of advice, introductions and sometimes investment. That group is a batch. Each programme has its own terms.

**Fellowship.** A programme that supports a person to study, research or build. Support may include a mentor, a stipend, or both. A stipend is money paid to support your participation.

**Research lab.** A team that tries to answer new technical questions. It may share papers, code and experiments. An open-source community maintains software that others can use and improve under its licence. A company builds and sells a product. People often work across all three.

**State of the art, or SOTA.** The best demonstrated methods for a particular task at a particular time. Ask what was tested, on which data, and how well it worked.

[Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/) helped companies such as Airbnb and Stripe get started. Its free course, [Startup School](https://www.startupschool.org/), explains how people turn an idea into a useful product. Its [company directory](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies) lets you explore what teams are building. A batch label tells you when a company joined YC. Open three robotics or hardware companies and explain what each does in one sentence.

[Speedrun](https://speedrun.a16z.com/) is an accelerator run by the investment firm a16z. [Entrepreneur First](https://www.joinef.com/) helps people form companies, including meeting a co-founder: the person you start with. [Station F](https://stationf.co/) is a startup campus in Paris that hosts several programmes. These are different ways to meet collaborators and get support. Read each programme's requirements when you have a reason to apply.

Cities help people meet. San Francisco and the Bay Area are useful places to explore AI and startup companies; London for AI and science; Paris for AI and its startup campus; Munich for robotics and aerospace; New York for software, finance and media. You can begin from Sofia by reading their work, using their software and asking a thoughtful question.

Bulgaria is part of this world. Dronamics builds unmanned aircraft. EnduroSat builds satellites and their electronics. INSAIT in Sofia does AI research and runs a summer programme for undergraduates. The company section gives you a small set to explore first.

## 3. Your two-week drone challenge

### The Apricot Drone Hackathon

Build a system that surveys your aunt's orchard near Brno, helps a person inspect each tree, and ends with a clear bird's-eye map and a proposed treatment route.

The full [brief and starter kit](https://github.com/latintzar/apricot-drone-hackathon) explain the build steps, software, datasets and setup. A hackathon is a short, focused project with a deadline and a demonstration. This one lasts two weeks. Elena is welcome to join on a part she enjoys; credit each person's work.

### What the finished survey does

1. **Survey.** The aircraft follows a planned survey, with a pilot supervising, and takes overlapping photographs.
2. **Map.** The software joins the images into a bird's-eye map, called an orthomosaic. Each tree gets an ID, such as T01, that stays with its photographs.
3. **Inspect.** An image model points out visible concerns and suggests trees to look at more closely. The person approves or changes the selection.
4. **Look closer.** The drone takes close-ups of approved trees from suitable viewpoints. Each tree has a gallery. Tap an image to enlarge it. Choose flight paths and camera settings from the real aircraft's abilities and the space around the tree.
5. **Review.** The person reads the images and records which trees need another inspection or a treatment review. The model's suggestion and the person's decision are shown separately.
6. **Plan.** The final bird's-eye image shows numbered trees, the reviewed selections, and a proposed route through trees selected for treatment planning. Export the image and a file with the tree IDs, coordinates and decisions.

The route is a planning drawing. A future flight needs checks for obstacles, clearances, battery, positioning and permissions. Actual spraying is a later project with its own tests and approvals.

### What counts as finished

**Core submission.** A real image set becomes a map with numbered trees; each tree opens its images; a person can record inspection and treatment-review decisions; the map and proposed route export; someone else can repeat the recorded example on their laptop. Include a short video and a field note.

**Full survey challenge.** Add a documented automated survey flight and approved close-up flights, with the resulting images in the right galleries. This is the two-week aim once day one confirms the aircraft, operator and access. Software processing, manual collection and automated flight each get their own measured status.

If the aircraft or weather delays field work, complete the same software flow with a licensed public image set. Label its source and book the next field test. Show precisely which parts you tested.

**Next stage.** Improve the close-ups and test your image model on new trees. Then scope the treatment system: its permissions, pump, coverage, calibration, pilot override and records. Agree that scope after the survey works.

### Your schedule

**Days 1–2 — Find out what you have.** Confirm the drone model, camera, control interface, pilot credentials, orchard access and your uncle's availability. Publish one existing project. Watch the first three videos. Run the starter example. Output: a public repo, the hardware facts and the first image displayed.

**Days 3–5 — Make the map.** Collect or obtain an image set. Build the orthomosaic and mark the trees; add a tree detector if useful. Keep the tree IDs and image sources. Output: the first bird's-eye map and a field note.

**Days 6–9 — Build inspection.** Open a tree's gallery, add model observations, and save a person's decisions. Test a close-up mission in simulation, then in an approved field session when ready. Output: a working review flow and close-ups matched to trees.

**Days 10–12 — Finish the plan.** Draw the proposed route through selected trees. Export the map and decisions. Replay the full example. Give your uncle the short instructions and watch where he gets stuck. Output: a complete demonstration and one improvement from his feedback.

**Days 13–14 — Explain what you built.** Finish the README, record a two-minute demonstration, write a reflection and update LinkedIn. Choose three people to contact. Output: one link you are ready to share.

Use two focused work blocks each day, with time for learning and a short log entry. Arrange twenty-minute check-ins with Peter around days 3, 7 and 14. Bring the latest image, the main difficulty and your next experiment.

## 4. Put your work where people can see it

### GitHub: your work and how to use it

“This is what I built, how you can use it, and the experiments and field notes behind it.”

Git is a tool that records changes to files. GitHub is a website that hosts those projects. A repository, or repo, holds one project's code, instructions and history. A commit is a saved change with a short explanation. The README is the page visitors see first. Open source means others can use and change your code under its licence.

Create your own [GitHub account](https://github.com/signup). Choose a username you are happy to share professionally. Add a photo, a short description and a contact route. Turn on two-factor authentication. Explore the [Student Developer Pack](https://education.github.com/pack) with your university details; check the current offers when you need one.

Start with your strongest existing project. Explain what it does, your part, how to run it and what you learned. Add one real picture or short recording. Credit collaborators and the libraries you used. Then add your second project and the orchard challenge. Pin the projects you want visitors to see first.

Keep your drone README simple: the family problem; one image; what works today; how to run the example; results and failures; links to field notes and reflections; the next experiment. State the precise name of your qualification as written on the certificate. Keep certificate numbers and home locations private.

### LinkedIn: a clear introduction

“Here is what I study, what I can do, what I am building and the work I would like to join.”

Use the same name, photo and project descriptions as GitHub. Add your exact degree title, university, study dates, skills you have used, and the verified qualification name. Put project links in Featured and explain your contribution to each. Say when you are available for an internship or part-time work.

Example headline, to adapt to your exact course: “Technical University of Sofia student | AI, computing and drone projects.”

Example About: “I study [exact course] at the Technical University of Sofia. I have built [project] and am working on an orchard survey tool that links drone images to individual trees. I am interested in computer vision, robotics and field testing. I would like to learn with a team building systems used in the real world. My projects: [link].”

Use your own words. Short sentences make it easy for someone to understand you.

### Project page: a quick tour

“Here is the project in two minutes: the problem, the demonstration, the results and the code.”

A simple page on [GitHub Pages](https://pages.github.com/) is a useful next step. Put the video first, followed by a bird's-eye image, two or three measured results, your reflection and the repo link. You can use the address GitHub provides. A personal domain can come later. A live web demo is an optional addition once the recorded example works.

### Substack: your learning journal, optional

“Here is my journey on this project: what I tried, what surprised me, what I learned and what comes next. Each post links to the work.”

[Substack](https://substack.com/) hosts posts that people can read online or receive by email. If you enjoy explaining what you learn, write there after a useful milestone. You can also keep the same writing in your repo. Choose the place you will keep using.

A first post could be called “Building a drone survey for my aunt's orchard.” Explain why you started, show the aircraft or first image, say what works today and name the next question. A later post could be “What I learned from my first orchard map.” Add an image, one mistake and the change you made. Write your observations first; ask an agent to help make the English clear.

### What to record as you go

Keep a short log: date; what you tried; what happened; one measurement or observation; next step. Save the original images and results. For public posts, use images you have permission to share and remove sensitive location details.

At a milestone, write 150–300 words: What did I expect? What happened? What evidence changed my mind? What did I learn? What will I try next? Add one image with a caption. This becomes a record you can show an engineer, a friend or your family.

### Write a short email

Find a relevant engineer, team lead or founder through the company website, an article or its public GitHub. Use a published work address, the careers contact or a short LinkedIn message. Read enough to ask about one specific part of the work.

Example to fill with your real details:

“Hi [name], I read your page about [specific system]. I study [course] in Sofia and have been building an orchard survey tool. It currently [measured result], and here is the short demo: [link]. How does your team handle [one question from your experiment]? I would like to learn more about this work and contribute through an internship or a small project. Would you have twenty minutes for a call?”

Send three careful messages after the demo. Keep a private list with the person, company, question, date, reply and next step. One brief follow-up after a week is reasonable. Continue improving the project while you wait.

### On the call

Give a one-minute introduction and run the example. Show one result and one difficulty. Ask about the person's work, how students contribute and what skill would help you most. Agree one next step and a date. Send a short thank-you afterwards and follow through.

## 6. Keep learning during the year

### A small weekly routine

**Most days, ten minutes:** browse [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/), where people discuss software, science and projects. Open one item that interests you. Follow the original work behind the discussion.

**Once a week, thirty minutes:** read [The Recursive](https://therecursive.com/) for the regional startup scene or [Sifted](https://sifted.eu/) for European companies. [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/) covers technology companies more widely. Pick one source per session; follow an interesting company to its own product and careers pages.

**Once a week, one learning block:** take the next Startup School talk, read a technical article or paper, or reproduce a small example. [Import AI](https://importai.substack.com/) is a weekly research newsletter. [Hugging Face Papers](https://huggingface.co/papers) helps you discover papers. Choose one related to a question you already have.

**Once a week, one conversation or contribution:** ask a precise question in your drone community, share a result, improve instructions or help reproduce a bug. Include your setup, what you tried and the result. A pull request is a proposed code or documentation change for others to review.

**Once a month:** publish one reflection, refresh your project links and choose a company or researcher to learn about. Follow the [new YC companies](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies) and [YC jobs](https://www.workatastartup.com/) when you are exploring opportunities.

### See what the field is building

Pick one company or lab a month. Watch a demonstration, read how it works and write down a question. Start with [Boston Dynamics](https://bostondynamics.com/), [Skydio](https://www.skydio.com/) or [Physical Intelligence](https://www.physicalintelligence.company/) for robotics. Explore [Google DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/), [Anthropic research](https://www.anthropic.com/research) and [NVIDIA research](https://research.nvidia.com/) for AI. Their papers and code let you look beyond the demonstration.

Lex Fridman and [Dwarkesh Patel](https://www.dwarkesh.com/) host longer conversations with researchers and people building companies. Choose guests whose work you want to understand. Keep a question from each conversation and look for an experiment or paper that helps answer it.

### Programmes to explore

[INSAIT SURF](https://insait.ai/surf/) offers summer research for undergraduates. [Google Summer of Code](https://summerofcode.withgoogle.com/) supports mentored open-source work. Start with the project's community and a small contribution. Check the next published application cycle, eligibility and support on the official pages; put confirmed dates in your calendar.

For technical depth, [Stanford CS231n](https://cs231n.stanford.edu/) covers computer vision and [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/) provides university course materials. Pick the section that helps with your next experiment. Your university courses give you foundations to use here.

Over the year, aim to build a pattern: finish a useful piece, explain it clearly, get feedback and improve it. Each project helps you discover what kind of work you enjoy and who you would like to work with.

## 7. Use an agent as a tutor and project partner

Copy the block below into the coding tool you have access to. It gives the agent this guide and the challenge. Ask it to explain new terms, help install one tool at a time, review code and make your writing clearer.

```text
I am Ilian. Help me learn while I build the Apricot Drone Hackathon.
Read these two pages and my repository's instructions:
https://raw.githubusercontent.com/latintzar/apricot-drone-hackathon/main/WORLD.md
https://raw.githubusercontent.com/latintzar/apricot-drone-hackathon/main/README.md
Use short, clear English. Ask the hardware questions one at a time.
Start with the offline exercise, then one real image.
Our goal is a survey, galleries, human review and a proposed treatment route.
Explain changes, let me test them, and ask me to explain what I learned.
Record actual results and credit the tools and people involved.
First, tell me the next three small steps and ask the first hardware question.
```

You choose the problem, run the experiments, inspect the results and explain the decisions. When code is new to you, ask: “Walk me through one image from the camera to the map. Show me the files and explain what each step does.” Then change one thing and check what happens.

A useful session ends with something small you can show: a picture, a passing check, a clearer explanation or a question you can now answer.
